The question behind my work

Can the electric vehicles filling our roads make getting around better — and make the energy systems communities depend on stronger, not more strained?

01 — What I study

One vehicle, three roles

Switch between roles to see how the same battery serves travel, the grid, and a building in an outage — and what my published work has found about each.

Role 01 · A mobility resource

Every kilowatt-hour has a trip attached

Before a car or a school bus can help a building or the grid, it still has to get people where they need to go. My models carry daily travel needs through every energy decision — and ask who bears the cost of getting around in the first place.

Electric-vehicle adoption narrows spatial disparities in U.S. transportation energy vulnerability — but with diminishing returns over time.

Liu & Kontou, Sustainable Cities and Society, 2022

Role 02 · A flexible load

Charging when and where it helps

Most charging happens where people live and work. I pair charging with building operation — HVAC setpoints, daylighting, time-of-use prices — so vehicles soak up cheap, spare energy instead of adding to the peak.

Managing workplace charging together with office-building loads lowers charging bills compared with first-come, first-served charging.

Liu, Vlachokostas, Si & Kontou, Transportation Research Part D, 2024

Role 03 · A source of backup power

When the grid goes dark

With vehicle-to-home and vehicle-to-building technology, a parked battery can help carry a household — and supply critical loads at a school — through an outage, as long as we account for the trips it must still make and the heating or cooling the building needs.

Even at 50% charge, today’s EVs can carry a home through a 12-hour outage in spring and autumn in every U.S. climate region studied except the Midwest.

Liu, Vlachokostas & Kontou, Energy, 2025; on school buses, see Building and Environment, 2025
